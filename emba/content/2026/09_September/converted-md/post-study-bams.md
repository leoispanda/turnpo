## PDF 页 5
Where Firm Value Comes From
■ A firm begins with amission: the specific need it exists to serve, and for
whom. Maersk’s is to move its customers’ goods across the world, end to
end and on schedule.
■ Ambition sets the scale of that mission. How much of the market, how
quickly, and how much risk is acceptable on the way.
■ Strategy is the choice of how: which assets to own, which activities to run
in-house, which markets to enter, and which risks to carry rather than pass
on.
■ Executed well, this is simply how a firm makes a profit: charging more for
the service than it costs to provide, over enough volume, for long enough.
■ Those choices createvalue only when what they earn exceeds the cost of
the capital they consume.
■ Risk management exists to protect that value.

## PDF 页 6
Where You See It: the Balance Sheet and the P&L
■ Strategy is invisible until it shows up in two statements, and there are only
two.
■ The balance sheetis strategy instock form: the choices already made.
What the firm owns, and who funded it, equity and debt.
■ The P&L is strategy inflow form: what those choices earned over one
year. Revenue, the costs of serving it, and what survives to the bottom.
■ The two are joined. This year’s profit, less what is paid out, becomes next
year’s equity. A strategy that works compounds on the balance sheet.
■ We use one real firm,Maersk, to make each block concrete: what it owns,
how it is funded, what it earns, and what it is worth.

Maersk: Balance Sheet: Assets
■ Assets are everything the firm owns that has value: cash, debtors, stock, ships,
terminals, equipment.
■ Current assets turn into cash within a year: cash, debtors, stock. They show the firm’s
day-to-day cushion.
■ Fixed assets are kept for the long run, like vessels and terminals. They show what the
business is built on.

Maersk: Balance Sheet: Equity & Liabilities
■ Liabilities are money owed to others. Equity is what is left for shareholders.
■ Current liabilities are due within a year. Long-term debt is due later. Shareholder equity
is the cushion left for owners.

## PDF 页 11
What Happened in 2021–22?
■ Between 2020 and 2022 the balance sheet nearly doubled: total assets
e49.1bn to e89.0bn.
■ Almost all of the growth wascash: e5.1bn to e27.2bn, more than five
times over, while stocks and debtors barely moved.
■ Long-term debt was flat throughout,e11.6bn to e11.7bn, while
shareholder equity wente26.1bn to e60.8bn. The expansion was funded
entirely by retained profit, not by any financing decision.
■ The cause sits in the P&L. Post-pandemic demand met congested ports,
freight rates spiked, and profit went frome2.5bn in 2020 toe27.8bn in
2022.
■ It reversed just as fast. By 2023 revenue was back toe47.2bn, profit to
e3.6bn, and total assets toe75.9bn as cash was returned to shareholders.
■ The lesson: a balance sheet can double and halve again without a single
financing decision, operating risk.

## PDF 页 16
How Do We Know Whether a Firm Is Doing Well?
■ Compare ratios to its own history: improving or worsening?
■ Compare ratios to peers in the same sector and country.
■ Ask what the market thinks: share price, credit spreads.
■ Watch for hard warning signs: negative equity, a breached covenant, a
downgrade.
■ Maersk FY2024: solvency 65%, current ratio 2.4x, ROE 11%. Healthy,
despite last year’s earnings collapse.

## PDF 页 17
What Drives a Firm: Value and Risk
■ We now have both views of Maersk. The accounts answer what the firm
earned and owns at the last reporting date; the market answers what
investors expect it to earn next, and how sure they are of it.
■ Value comes from what the firm earns.Risk comes from how far those
earnings can move.
■ Maersk shows how far that can be: earnings ofe27.8bn in 2022,e3.6bn
in 2023, on a balance sheet that barely changed.
■ Movement on that scale only becomes a problem when it threatens the
firm’s ability to meet its obligations.

## PDF 页 18
What We Mean by Distress
■ Distress is not bankruptcy. Bankruptcy is a legal event. Distress is an
economic condition that usually comes first, often by years.
■ Working definition.A firm is in financial distress when it can no longer
meet its obligations on the terms it agreed, so that its strategy can no
longer be fully executed.
■ We will separate four states properly in the next section:healthy, distress,
default, bankrupt. Most firms that enter distress never reach the last two.
■ Risk, value deterioration and the accompanying costs begin at distress, not
at default.

## PDF 页 20
Distress is Costly
■ Financial distress is not the same as economic distress. A firm can have a
sound business and still be in serious trouble, purely because of how it is
financed.
■ Andrade & Kaplan test exactly that case: highly leveraged firms whose
operations were healthy but whosebalance sheetswere not. What is lost
there is down to leverage, not to a failing business.
■ Distress alone costs about 10% of firm value on average, up to 25%
(Andrade & Kaplan, 1998).
■ Most of that loss is a decline in the business itself. Customers go
elsewhere, suppliers tighten terms, good people leave, and investment
stops. A financing problem becomes a trading problem, with lost sales and
foregone projects.

Shareholder Perspective

Variability in Practice: Vestas
■ Vestas, the world’s largest wind turbine maker, went from earnings of
e176m in FY2021 to a loss ofe1.57bn in FY2022. On revenue of
e14.5bn that is a margin of+1.1% turning to−10.9%.
■ The cause: fixed-price turbine contracts signed before steel, resin, copper

■ Recovery took years: earnings of onlye76m in FY2023, a 0.5% margin,
and e494m in FY2024, still under 3%.
■ This is exactly the variability the last slide warned about. It can push a
firm toward distress well before it goes broke.

## PDF 页 38
How Big Are the Bankruptcy Costs?
■ Direct costs are small and well measured.Lawyers, courts and advisers come to 4% of
market value in Warner’s railroad bankruptcies, 4.3% in Altman’s sample and 3.1% in
Weiss’s: an average of 3 to 4.5% of the firm’s market value one year before filing. The
worst single cases reach 6.6% and 9.8%.
■ If the firm is wound up rather than reorganised the bill is larger, 7.5% of the liquidated
value (Ang, Chua & McConnell, 1982).
■ Settling privately is roughly ten times cheaper.A debt restructuring agreed directly
with lenders costs a median 0.32% of assets (Gilson, John & Lang, 1990). All of these are
collected in Wruck (1990), Table 1.
■ The indirect costs are the ones that matter.Once the court is involved, it interferes
with investment and operating decisions. Add distress overall and the total reaches about
10% of firm value, up to 25% (Andrade & Kaplan, 1998).

## PDF 页 39
Bankruptcy Cost in Practice: Atlantia
■ On 14 August 2018 the Morandi Bridge in Genoa collapsed, killing 43 people. It was operated
by ASPI, the motorway company Atlantia owned.
■ ASPI’s whole business was a government licence to run a large part of Italy’s motorways and
keep the toll money. Within days the government said it would take that licence away.
■ The market did the arithmetic immediately.In that one month Atlantia’s equity fell from
e20.8bn toe14.7bn:minuse6.1bn, or 29%. Its equity volatility doubled, 17% to 34% a
year. Its distance to default fell from 8.2 to 1.9 standard deviations and the market-implied
probability of default went from effectively zero to 2.8%.
■ No court had ruled on anything. Nobody had filed for anything. This is the first bullet of the
Bankruptcy costslide, in numbers: thepossibilityof future bankruptcy costs is priced today.
■ It kept going. In March 2020 the distance to default wentnegative,−1.7, with an implied
probability of default of 95%. In 2021 Atlantia sold its 88% stake in ASPI for aboute9.3bn and
gave the business up.

Altman (1968): The Z-Score
■ Edward Altman was the first to make ratio analysis rigorous. Before him, analysts
eyeballed ratios one at a time. He combined five of them into a single number using
discriminant analysis.
Z = 1.2X1 +1.4X2 +3.3X3 +0.6X4 +1.0X5
■ X1 working capital / total assets (liquidity).
■ X2 retained earnings / total assets (accumulated profitability).
■ X3 earnings before interest and tax / total assets (current profitability).
■ X4 market value of equity / book value of total debt (leverage).
■ X5 sales / total assets (how hard the assets are working).
■ Trained on 66 US manufacturing firms, half bankrupt, half not. BelowZ = 1.81:
classified bankrupt. AboveZ = 2.67: classified safe. Between the two, Altman’s own
“zone of ignorance,” where the model itself is unsure.

Gearing and Solvency: Two Ratios or One?

never enter gearing.

Why an Equity Index Informs Credit Risk
■ The claim. A shareholder is paid only after the lenders, with a floor at
zero. That payoff is a call option on the firm’s assets, struck at the debt.
■ The volatility is the shared input.A lender needs to know how far the
asset value can move before it falls below what is owed. That range is not
observable. Equity volatility is, every day, and it is the levered image of it.
■ The timing is the advantage.Accounts are filed once a year. That
volatility line re-prices every month, and in 2008 it had trebled before the
year-end accounts were drafted.
■ So: what a shareholder reads as return and risk, a lender reads as distance
from default.

Distance to Default: What the Equation Is Really
Saying
■ Distance-to-default asks one plain question: how many standard deviations of asset-value
swings sit between the firm’s assets today and the point where debt would exceed them?
■ A distance of 15 means it would take a huge, once-in-a-generation shock to
wipe out the cushion – very safe
■ A distance below 1 means ordinary month-to-month market swings could
already be enough
■ Because it is built from market prices, it moves in real time – weeks or months before
the same deterioration would show up in filed accounts

## PDF 页 127
From Diagnosis to Action: The Management Toolkit
■ A solvency problemcalls for capital-structure action.
■ Retain what you earn: cut or suspend the dividend and the buyback.
■ Raise equity while the market is still open to you, not once it is not.
■ Sell assets and repay debt: Atlantia sold ASPI, E.ON exited Uniper.
■ A liquidity problemcalls for working-capital and financing-terms action.
■ Refinance long: replace what is due this year with what is due in five.
■ Arrange standby facilities before you need them: Wienerberger’se429m.
■ Collect faster and pay slower: tighten receivables, stretch payables.
■ A profitability problemcalls for operating action.
■ Reprice, where the customer will bear it: Vestas repriced its new contracts.
■ Take out fixed cost: Wienerberger closed 27 plants and idled 11 lines.
■ Exit what does not cover its capital. No financing move fixes a business that no longer
earns.
■ A market-risk driver– commodity, FX, rates – calls for hedging.
■ First name the exposure, and ask whether the firm is paid to carry it.
■ Then match the instrument to it: forwards, futures, swaps, options.
■ Hedge early, while the firm is still safe and the cover is still cheap.

■ Equity is the risk-management instrument of last resort.
■ Futures, forwards, swaps and options cover a great many risks.
■ For a bridge collapse or an expropriation there is no contract to buy.
