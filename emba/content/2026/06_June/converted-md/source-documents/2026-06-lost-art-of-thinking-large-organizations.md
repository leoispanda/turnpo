# The Lost Art Of Thinking In Large Organizations

## 1. One-Line Summary

The article argues that execution success can weaken strategic thinking unless leaders deliberately protect time for reflection, use-case reasoning, and delegation.

## 2. Why This Belongs To June 2026

This reading is a mental warm-up before the EMBA begins. It helps define what Leo should bring into the programme: not just a desire for frameworks, but a practice of strategic thought.

## 3. Core Argument

Large organizations often reward managers for getting things done. That bias toward execution can become a liability when those managers move into roles where they need to choose markets, define boundaries, and reason under uncertainty.

Strategic thinking is not just "finding a solution quickly." It is the work of deciding:

- which markets or opportunities are attractive;
- which ones should be avoided;
- which assumptions change the decision;
- where the boundary of the strategy lies.

## 4. Method: Start Narrow, Think Deeply

The useful strategic-thinking pattern:

1. Start with a concrete customer or use case.
2. Ask how the organization could create and capture value.
3. Change one assumption.
4. Watch whether the answer changes.
5. Use the changes to find strategic boundaries.

The point is not exhaustive coverage. The point is depth, ambiguity tolerance, and boundary discovery.

## 5. Leadership Implication

Delegation is not only a workload tactic. It is a strategic capability because it creates time for thought. If the leader never delegates enough to think, the organization stays trapped in execution reflexes.

The reading also makes the case for protected thinking routines. The example of a dedicated thinking retreat is useful less as a calendar template and more as a principle: strategic insight needs deliberate space.

## 6. Leo Work Application

Before each EMBA module:

- write one real strategic question from Turnpo, MapKAI, DishKAI, or current work;
- identify the concrete user, customer, or stakeholder case behind it;
- list three assumptions that could change the conclusion;
- ask what market, product, or leadership boundary becomes clearer;
- delegate or defer one execution task to protect thinking time.
