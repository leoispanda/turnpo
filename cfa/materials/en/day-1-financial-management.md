## Part 1 | What we are learning

### Knowledge fable | The second navigation chart

The Grey Harbour Merchants decided to build a large ship called North Star. The shipbuilder promised that it could carry the cargo of three old ships. If the northern route stayed prosperous, timber, iron and grain would create a mountain of silver within five years.

He drew an attractive forecast: build in year one, sail in year two, earn from year three, recover the investment in year four and receive a tide of silver thereafter. “If it will earn so much, why not start now?” the merchants asked. Only the old accountant Eli did not raise his hand. He rolled up the chart and said: “This shows what the ship looks like after it succeeds. Tomorrow I will bring a second navigation chart.”

The next morning Eli put an old parchment on the table. It had two columns: **do not build North Star** and **build North Star**. He removed last year’s exploration cost. It had been spent and could not be recovered, so it was a **sunk cost**. He added the rent the old shipyard could have earned from repairing other ships: no cash would leave the purse, but using the yard meant giving up that opportunity cost. He placed timber, wages, port fees, repairs, inventory and the eventual sale of the old hull in the years when they would really occur.

“Do not ask how much silver the ship brings in total,” Eli said. “Ask what Grey Harbour gains and loses because we build it.” The merchants saw that a project is judged by changed cash, not by a story or total revenue.

Eli then placed five identical silver coins at different distances. The nearest could buy timber today; the farthest would arrive in five years after storms, war, falling prices or cancelled orders might intervene. He replaced the farthest coin with a smaller copper coin. Future money had to pass through time and risk before being compared with money today. This was **discounting**. The required return could not be lowered just to make the project pass.

North Star still had a positive NPV at a reasonable discount rate. The merchants were excited, but Eli brought a third chart containing only dates: timber payment in March, wages in June, port and insurance in September, debt repayment the following January, and the first voyage only in summer. The purse could be empty before the ship earned anything. Debt could supply money but demanded interest, principal and covenants. Equity avoided fixed repayment but shared future earnings and control. Financing was therefore not about finding the cheapest money; it was about using money that let the association survive until value arrived.

The merchants argued between building the largest ship and abandoning the idea. Eli proposed a third choice: build a smaller standard ship for signed customers, learn the real costs and route, and approve expansion only if deliveries, cash and orders met pre-set conditions. A smaller commitment bought the right to make a better next decision. This was staging and a **real option**.

The merchants also worried about timber, iron, grain and foreign-currency prices. Eli put shields beside cargoes that already existed: real exposures could be hedged. He left an empty square without cargo. Locking a price for cargo that might never exist would turn the shield into a bet. A hedge should match amount, timing and settlement currency. The association could transfer interest-rate, currency and commodity exposures when it had no special advantage in bearing them, but it had to retain the core risks of ship choice, customers, service and operations.

Finally Eli drew the whole fleet. Three existing ships used different cargoes and served different customers, but all passed through Black Stone Strait, relied on the same northern bank and needed the same navigators. Their debts matured in the same winter. “You think you have three bets,” he said. “You have one bet on the same northern wind.”

The association kept most cargo on the cheap northern route but sent a small Lantern ship south every month. It maintained contracts, berths, warehouses, insurance and customs documents. The route was more expensive and sometimes unprofitable, but it was real backup capacity. When war closed Black Stone Strait, other merchants discovered that southern berths were full, insurance had tripled and nobody knew the reefs. Grey Harbour expanded Lantern from one ship to three, five and ten. It lost money, but it did not stop building, breach its debt or lose every customer. The old cost had purchased the ability to move when others were stuck.

After the war Eli placed four wooden stamps on the table: **invest**, **stage**, **delay** and **reject**. Build when value, cash, financing and risk conditions are satisfied; stage when direction is promising but information is missing; delay when the asset is valuable but cash, people or route are not ready; reject when it destroys value or exposes the whole association to an unbearable risk. The association approved a smaller trial ship with funding and hedges for signed cargo, while retaining the southern route. North Star would advance only when orders, cash, costs and routes met the written thresholds.

> **A shield beside real cargo is protection; a shield without cargo is a gamble.**
> **A route never used is not a fallback.**
> **The greatest danger is not using the northern route; it is being able to use only the northern route.**

### The judgement to learn today

Financial Management is not a sequence of NPV, debt and hedging formulas. Put four questions in one decision: does the project create **incremental cash flow** and positive NPV at a reasonable **discount rate**; can liquidity, financing capacity and covenant headroom carry the company to value realisation; which exposures should be hedged and which core risks must remain inside; and does the project increase portfolio concentration? The conclusion should state the assumptions, financing path, survival point and threshold that would change the decision.

## Part 2 | Five-step knowledge chain

> **Identify what the project changes → decide whether the change creates value → check whether the company can survive to collect it → protect the path against damaging risk → recommend invest, stage, delay or reject.**

### 1. Strip the project out of the statements and keep only changed cash

Compare the future with and without the project. The difference is incremental cash flow. Exclude irrecoverable research already paid; include a facility’s forgone rent, inventory, receivables and any opportunity cost. Accounting profit can point to income and cost, but it cannot replace the cash timeline.

### 2. Put different dates on one table and test value creation

Today’s euro and a euro in five years cannot be added directly. Discounting brings future cash back to today using a required return for comparable risk. Do not choose a low rate merely to pass the project. Positive NPV means the project is valuable under current assumptions; it is not proof that volume, price, cost and timing will hold.

### 3. After value, ask whether the company can stay alive until value arrives

Map equipment payments, inventory, wages, interest and customer receipts. A company can fail from a short-term cash shortage despite long-term value. Debt brings fixed payments, maturity and covenants; equity brings more buffer but shares returns and control. Compare minimum cash, covenant headroom and refinancing timing. Staging, delaying or preserving exit rights can be a rational real option.

### 4. Protect the funding path rather than smoothing every fluctuation

Risk management should prevent a bad scenario from cutting cash and financing capacity at the critical moment. Customer, product, technology and operating risks often create the firm’s return and are core risks. Interest, currency and commodity exposures may be non-core and transferable through forwards, options, swaps, insurance or natural hedges. State the exposure, transmission to cash, protection threshold and counterparty risk before hedging.

### 5. Put the project back into the enterprise and decide

A good stand-alone project may share a customer, currency, supplier or funding channel with existing activities. Use a portfolio view to examine cash, credit, risk capacity, concentration and correlation. The final recommendation must include value, financing route, residual risk and the threshold for a changed decision.

### Reading 1 | Berk, DeMarzo & Harford (2025)

* Assigned book: *Fundamentals of Corporate Finance*, 6th Global Edition.
* ISBN 9781292470047; eISBN 9781292738048.
* The local fourth-edition summary covers time value, NPV, capital budgeting, risk and cost of capital, debt/equity and real options. It does not replace the 2025 sixth-edition text or formal page citations.
* Assigned sections: Chapter 1 §§1.3–1.5; Chapter 3 §§3.3–3.4; Chapter 4; Chapter 6 §§6.1–6.2; Chapter 7 §7.1; Chapters 10, 20 and 21.
* Financial markets, investor expectations, risk-return, diversification and derivatives also appear in lecture outcomes.

[Open the Berk fourth-edition summary PDF](../2026-09/readings/berk-demarzo-fourth-edition-summary.pdf)

#### Reading frame

1. **Decision owner**: What investment, financing and cash decisions belong to the financial manager?
2. **Cash-flow valuation**: How are incremental cash flows identified and discounted?
3. **Markets and expectations**: How do expectations enter required return, security prices and financing terms?
4. **Risk-return and diversification**: What risk can diversification reduce, and what remains?
5. **Financial claims**: How do bonds, equity and enterprise value connect?
6. **Short-term survival**: How can long-term value and liquidity be judged together?
7. **Flexibility and derivatives**: Which real options are preserved, and how do derivatives reshape exposure?

**Summary**: Berk supplies the language of Day 1. Firm value comes from future cash, not accounting profit alone; claims have different priority; and positive NPV still has to pass a liquidity test. Uncertainty can be addressed by discount rates and by staged investment that preserves options.

### Reading 2 | Stulz (1996), “Rethinking Risk Management”

[Open the Stulz PDF](../2026-09/readings/Rethinking-Risk-Management-1cnhar7.pdf)

* **The puzzle**: if shareholders can diversify themselves, why does the company need risk management?
* **Value mechanism**: downside can create financing constraints and underinvestment.
* **Risk selection**: keep core risks tied to comparative advantage; transfer non-core risks at reasonable cost.
* **Hedge governance**: a hedge ratio changed from a market view can become speculation; use mandate, benchmark and limits.
* **Decision threshold**: focus on losses that threaten investment capacity.

**Summary**: Risk management is not the promise to reduce every fluctuation. It can create value by protecting the capacity to fund positive-NPV projects when external finance becomes expensive or unavailable.

### Reading 3 | Nocco & Stulz (2006), “Enterprise Risk Management: Theory and Practice”

[Open the Nocco & Stulz journal PDF](../2026-09/readings/Nocco-Stulz-2006-Enterprise-Risk-Management-Journal.pdf)

* **Macro ERM** protects enterprise capital, financing and strategic execution.
* **Micro ERM** makes managers consider risk in each decision.
* **Portfolio view** reveals shared currency, customer or supplier dependence.
* **Capital allocation** prioritises risks worth taking when cash and financing are limited.

Today’s stopping point is the enterprise bridge. Formal risk appetite, categories, COSO components, Board/CRO governance and implementation belong to Day 3.

**Summary**: A project must explain not only its own NPV but also how it uses enterprise cash, borrowing capacity and risk capacity, and whether it adds concentration to the portfolio.
