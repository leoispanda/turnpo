## Part 1 | What we are learning

### Knowledge fable | Three whiteboards in the pizza kitchen

Beside a university town stood a small pizza shop called One Slice on the Corner. It had six tables, but delivery made up half of its business. On Friday evenings the printer never stopped, and owner Amin felt business was excellent.

At the end of March he told his wife that profit was 30% above the same month last year. She asked whether they had sold more pizza. They had not. The increase came from selling an old delivery scooter for €4,000. Because its book value was already low, the gain appeared as current profit. The gain was real, but there would be no scooter to sell next March.

When Amin looked around the shop, the freezer door no longer closed properly and the oven heated slowly. The Friday shift had been reduced from four people to three. Sales had not increased, but negative delivery reviews were rising.

He asked Zhou to make a risk table. Oven failure was yellow, expired ingredients yellow, staff absence and late delivery red, and a lower platform rating yellow. The table was soon covered by schedules, old menus and drink posters. Nobody noticed when risks changed colour.

The failure arrived on another Friday when a stadium event sent orders soaring. A worker called in sick, only two bags of cheese remained and the oven temperature became unstable. Zhou asked when to close one delivery platform and whether dine-in or delivery should come first. Amin said, “Let’s see” and “Do not delay either.” Those sounded like answers but made no decision.

By seven o’clock some pizzas were burned at the edge and raw in the middle. Customers demanded refunds, but no one knew who could pause orders. More than twenty orders were refunded and the platform rating fell from 4.7 to 4.2. One student wrote: “The pizza tastes the same, but everyone is busy and nobody is really deciding.”

The next morning Amin replaced the posters and risk table with three whiteboards.

The first asked: **How did we actually make money this month?** Profit was split into pizza operations and the scooter sale. Platform receivables, unpaid supplier bills, used ingredients and refunded orders became visible. The gain was correct, but it did not prove that the pizza business had improved. Amin separated “are the numbers recorded correctly?” from “what do they mean?”

The second asked: **What could stop us from making a good pizza?** Two oven temperature failures required a same-day check. Cheese for fewer than 100 pizzas stopped promotions. Forty open orders let the shift manager pause one platform. A Friday team of fewer than four could not accept a large group order. A rating below 4.5 required next-day review. “Colour is not for a complete-looking table,” Amin said. “A changed colour must change a practice.”

The third asked: **Who can decide?** Front-of-house gave honest waiting times and could refund within a limit. The kitchen lead decided whether ingredients and pizzas were safe. The shift manager could pause orders without waiting for Amin. Zhou checked indicators but was not only a colour updater. Amin’s wife reconciled platform settlements, supplier bills and physical reality each week.

For two months profit looked less attractive. The scooter gain did not recur, deferred repairs were booked, refunds reduced revenue, and extra staffing raised cost. “It was not as good as I thought,” Amin said. “At least now you know where the problem is,” his wife replied.

When the stadium held another event, the manager closed one platform at 42 open orders. When the oven temperature moved outside its range, the kitchen stopped one rack and updated the waiting time. They accepted fewer orders but avoided mass refunds and new complaints. Amin said, “We earned a little less tonight.” The manager replied, “But we can open tomorrow.”

The three boards later acquired formal names. **Financial Accounting** shows recognised revenue, cost, assets and obligations. **Enterprise Risk Management (ERM)** identifies uncertainty that could affect objectives and sets indicators, action lines and responses. **Governance** clarifies who owns operations, who can stop work, who challenges and who tests effectiveness.

> Good news in the books must be checked in the shop; bad news on the wall needs someone to act on it.

### The judgement to learn today

Financial Accounting, ERM and Governance are one management process:

> **See what happened → understand the numbers → identify what could affect objectives → set action thresholds → define who decides, challenges and checks.**

Ask three basic questions before memorising terminology: Do the numbers reflect operating reality? What could affect the organisation’s objectives? When a problem appears, who has both responsibility and authority to act?

## Part 2 | Five-step knowledge chain

### 1. How one sale enters three statements

The **income statement** records recognised revenue, cost and profit over a period. The **balance sheet** records what the company owns, owes and retains at a point in time. The **cash-flow statement** records cash actually received and paid.

If a delivery platform sells a €20 pizza using €8 of ingredients but pays next month, the income statement can show €20 revenue, €8 cost and €12 profit. The balance sheet shows a €20 receivable and €8 less inventory. Cash flow shows no €20 receipt yet. Profit and cash differ without the accounting being wrong.

### 2. Correct numbers can have more than one explanation

Higher profit may come from stronger operations or a one-off asset sale. Higher receivables may show growth or slower customers. More inventory may prepare for a peak or signal weak sales. A carrying value does not prove that an asset still works or is worth the same. Ask whether a change is recurring, whether accounting value matches reality and what evidence supports the interpretation. EBITDA, margin, working capital, provision and impairment help ask questions; they do not answer them automatically.

### 3. ERM starts with objectives, not a risk list

ERM is not a risk department maintaining a spreadsheet. Operations owns equipment, staffing and delivery; Quality owns food and product quality; Finance owns cash, budget and funding; IT owns systems and data; business and Compliance share compliance responsibility. The organisation first defines its objective—safe quality, timely delivery and customer trust—then identifies uncertainty, owners, monitoring and action.

### 4. Give risks signals, limits and responses

**Risk appetite** is the amount and type of risk the organisation is willing to accept. A **KRI** shows risk increasing before loss. A **threshold** says when action is mandatory. A **risk owner** monitors and responds. Forty open orders can be the KRI threshold, pausing a delivery platform the response, and the shift manager the owner. Colour alone is not management.

### 5. Governance says who owns, challenges and checks

The **first line** runs the business and owns daily risk. The **second line**—Risk, Compliance, Finance or Quality—sets expertise, advises and challenges without taking over the business decision. The **third line**, usually Internal Audit, independently assesses design and operation. A Board or supervisory body sets objectives, boundaries and escalation expectations. In a non-profit or public organisation the objective may be safety, fairness, response time or public trust rather than profit, but budgets, evidence and accountability still matter.

### Fable correspondence

| Story event | Management concept | Plain meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Profit rose after the scooter sale | One-off gain | Higher profit does not necessarily mean core operations improved. |
| Platform payment had not arrived and supplier was unpaid | Profit versus cash | Recognition and cash payment occur at different times. |
| Posters covered the risk table | Risk register is not ERM | Recording a risk is not managing it. |
| Forty-two orders triggered a platform pause | KRI, threshold, response | A boundary should trigger a pre-agreed action. |
| Everyone asked but nobody decided | Governance | Unclear authority leaves problems untreated. |
| Fewer orders prevented refunds and complaints | Resilience | A short-term sacrifice can protect delivery capability and trust. |

### Analogy boundary

Three whiteboards do not replace complex recognition, measurement, consolidation, risk models or directors’ legal duties. COSO provides reasonable assurance, not a guarantee that risk will not occur. Public purpose does not remove opportunity cost or prioritisation.

### Apply it to your organisation

Choose a KPI management sees often and write five lines:

1. **Fact**: Which statement or operating record produces it?
2. **Judgement**: What estimate, definition or misreading does it contain?
3. **Objective**: Which strategic goal or public mission does it affect?
4. **Trigger**: Which threshold requires action?
5. **Accountability**: Who owns action, who challenges and who verifies closure?

### Core vocabulary

| Term | Plain-English meaning |
|---|---|
| **accrual** | Record an economic event when it occurs, not when cash moves. |
| **matching** | Recognise costs in the period whose revenue they help create. |
| **revenue recognition** | Decide when and at what amount revenue is recorded. |
| **working capital** | Net operating cash tied up in receivables, inventory and payables. |
| **provision** | Estimate for an obligation with uncertain timing or amount. |
| **impairment** | Recognition that an asset’s recoverable value has fallen. |
| **governance** | Arrangements for oversight, decisions, challenge and accountability. |
| **oversight** | Supervisory review and challenge of management. |
| **risk appetite** | Risk type and amount accepted to pursue objectives. |
| **risk owner** | Person accountable for a risk and its response. |
| **KRI** | Key Risk Indicator showing risk accumulation before loss. |
| **threshold** | Boundary at which action becomes mandatory. |
| **three lines** | First line owns, second line challenges, third line independently assesses. |
| **reasonable assurance** | Risk reduced to a reasonable level, never zero error. |
| **accountability** | Explaining and taking responsibility for decisions and results. |

### The reading line

COSO connects ERM to strategy and performance; Grant Thornton turns principles into an organisational framework; Deloitte adds non-financial-risk taxonomy, culture and three lines; DSM tests how much a disclosure can support. The shared judgement is: **good governance does not mean risk never occurs; it means facts are recorded, risks are seen in time, and someone must act and be tested.**
