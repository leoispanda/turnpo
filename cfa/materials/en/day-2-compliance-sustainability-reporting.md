# Day 2 | Compliance, Sustainability Reporting & Leadership in Finance

> **Core question**: When growth pressure begins to distort behaviour and data, how can a company see facts in time, stop the wrong action, and prove that its commitments are credible?

## Part 1 | What we are learning

### Knowledge fable | The clean checklist

At the corner of an old street stood a small restaurant called Anan’s Kitchen. It had eight tables, sold lunch boxes at noon and served home-style food in the evening. Anan often said, “Our reputation depends on food that is clean, safe and served quickly.”

To make the promise visible, he put a checklist by the kitchen door with three sections: fresh ingredients, kitchen hygiene and customer waiting time below fifteen minutes. Before closing, the employee on duty ticked each section. Three checkmarks meant the day had gone well.

At first the checklist helped. Someone checked the fridge temperature, cleaned oil from the floor and pushed the kitchen during the delivery rush. Business grew, and Anan won corporate lunch contracts. He then asked the kitchen to prepare 300 extra lunch boxes each day.

One morning cook Zhou noticed that chicken delivered the previous day smelled slightly sour. It should have been returned or isolated for the owner to inspect, but 200 chicken-rice boxes were due at a corporate client that noon. Zhou moved the box to a corner “to handle tonight”. During the rush a new helper used some of it. That evening “fresh ingredients” was still ticked: nobody wanted to write down the problem, and no one knew whether setting it aside counted as handling it.

Li, who ran delivery, faced another conflict. Oil and water covered the floor. The rule said to stop and clean when the floor became slippery, but stopping would slow service, trigger complaints and lower the platform rating. Li put up a caution sign and continued. A helper slipped and scratched his arm. Li said it was not serious and asked him not to file an accident report. At closing, “kitchen hygiene” was ticked because the floor had eventually been cleaned.

Soon customers reported stomach discomfort and two negative reviews said the food was not fresh. Zhou showed Anan the checklist. Every section was green, so the team said there was no problem. Meanwhile, more ingredients were put aside, floor incidents were not logged, injuries went unrecorded and complaints were not linked to ingredient batches.

At month-end Anan used the clean checklist to pursue a larger corporate client. Before the contract was signed, market inspectors arrived. They asked for purchase records, fridge-temperature logs, accident records, complaints, ingredient handling and corrective-action records. Anan realised that many boxes were ticked without evidence behind them.

The inspectors asked: “If everything was compliant, why is there no record of what you did after problems happened?” Anan had no answer. The restaurant closed for one day of internal remediation. The staff complained about lost revenue and slower service. Anan finally understood that the rules had become decoration: everyone protected speed and revenue, but no one made exceptions visible or owned their closure.

The new checklist recorded five things for every exception: where it came from, when it was found, who handled it, who reviewed the result and when the next step would be completed. Strange-smelling food had to be isolated or returned and traced to dishes already sold. A slippery floor stopped the affected area until cleaning was verified. An injury triggered a process review. A customer complaint was traced to batch, temperature and dish.

If the same issue appeared three times in a week, the kitchen reduced orders. If food safety could not be confirmed, the dish stopped selling. If a corporate order made safety checks impossible, Anan rejected the order rather than accept “we were too busy”.

The first week looked worse: red records multiplied, service slowed, the platform rating fell briefly and Anan lost a large client. But the records became credible. Ingredients had batch records, staff knew what to report, complaints could be traced, and somebody owned and reviewed corrective action. Inspectors returned and said: “This record is not pretty, but it is credible.”

Anan kept the promise “clean, safe and served quickly” but added: “Real compliance is not ticking every box; it is stopping and handling problems when they appear.” He later called the evidence chain Sustainability Reporting and the willingness to accept short-term revenue loss to fix a problem Financial Leadership.

### The judgement to learn today

Policies, published reports and system launches are not final evidence. A credible operating chain turns legal, regulatory, ethical and public commitments into boundaries; traces pressure, behaviour, exceptions, information and harm; tests whether controls operate; links each ESG claim to data, method, owner and review; and gives bad news the power to change budgets, speed and resources. **A credible report is not always green; it is a report that makes red lights actionable.**

## Part 2 | Five-step knowledge chain

### 1. Put promises and non-negotiable lines on the table

Start with obligations and commitments. Translate them into observable boundaries: do not transact with sanctioned clients; do not push pollution outside the value chain; report safety incidents within the required time. Growth, delivery speed and sales incentives are not automatically bad, but ask whether they push people toward crossing a line.

### 2. Trace the behaviour chain after an exception

When a violation, false green number or suppressed complaint appears, ask more than “who did it?” Trace the pressure, changed behaviour, bypassed control, delayed or beautified information and resulting harm. Personal accountability and organisational analysis belong together. Find the earliest point at which the organisation could have stopped the harm.

### 3. Test whether a control exists or operates

A policy, training session or software launch proves design, not operation. **Design effectiveness** asks whether the control could detect and handle the issue. **Operating effectiveness** asks whether it is executed, logged, investigated, escalated and closed. Check backlogs, high-risk samples, overrides, repeat issues and original evidence instead of accepting “we have an AML process”.

### 4. Pull each ESG statement back to evidence

For every disclosure ask: why is the issue material, where in the value chain does the impact occur, which boundary and baseline are used, who records and reviews it, how are targets set, and how are changes explained? **Double materiality** sees both how company activity affects people and the environment and how those effects influence cash flow, financing, reputation and continuity. Assurance cannot create evidence that management never retained.

### 5. Let bad news change the pace of business

Reliable information can hurt short-term results: pausing a risky client reduces revenue, re-testing delays delivery, and remediation costs money. Financial leadership defines thresholds, stop authority, correction deadlines and independent closure. If risk capacity is exhausted, growth speed must be adjustable. That is tone from the top in practice.

### Analogy boundary

The restaurant does not replace AML procedures, ESRS datapoints or formal assurance. Double materiality requires a systematic method and stakeholder evidence. Red records do not automatically prove good management; timely completed action does.

### Apply it to your organisation

Choose a process or ESG metric that looks normal and write five lines:

1. **Promise**: what has the company promised, and to whom?
2. **Pressure**: which growth, cost or speed target may distort behaviour?
3. **Hidden evidence**: which backlog, exception or boundary issue may be missing from reporting?
4. **Control proof**: what evidence would show that the control really operates?
5. **Authority**: which threshold gives someone the power to slow or stop the business?

### Core vocabulary

| Term | Plain-English meaning |
|---|---|
| **compliance** | Ability to keep meeting legal, regulatory, ethical and internal rules. |
| **control override** | An authorised person bypasses or overrules a control. |
| **operating effectiveness** | Whether a control executes consistently and correctly in real operations. |
| **beneficial owner** | Person who ultimately owns or controls a customer entity. |
| **alert backlog** | Triggered alerts not yet investigated and closed. |
| **escalation** | Passing an issue to a higher authority after a limit is exceeded. |
| **double materiality** | Assessing outward impact and financial impact together. |
| **impact materiality** | Whether the company’s effect on people and the environment is significant. |
| **financial materiality** | Whether an issue can significantly affect financial prospects. |
| **IRO** | Impacts, risks and opportunities. |
| **value chain** | Upstream, internal and downstream activities within scope. |
| **baseline** | Starting value used to measure progress or decline. |
| **assurance** | Independent checking that raises trust in disclosure and data process. |
| **greenwashing** | Selective or misleading information that exaggerates sustainability performance. |
| **tone from the top** | Behavioural standard communicated by senior leaders’ real decisions. |

### The reading line

COSO supplies the organisational frame; Volkswagen shows how targets and silence can defeat professional judgement; ING shows how backlog becomes legal and trust damage; Estra shows reporting as management; KPMG shows implementation problems at ESRS boundaries. Together: **credible operations depend on making exceptions visible, owned and closed.**
