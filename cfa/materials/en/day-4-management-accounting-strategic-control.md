## Part 1 | What we are learning

### Knowledge fable | The third spoon of chilli oil

On the riverside stood a century-old noodle shop called Old Sichuan Notes. Its signature dish was a bowl of noodles with chilli oil: springy noodles, deep aroma and heat without harshness. Every morning at five, the master cook prepared three pots of oil, selecting three kinds of chilli and four stages of heat. The fragrance travelled halfway down the street.

A new owner eventually took over. He had graduated from a leading university and was fluent in modern management. His first act was to install a “precision operating-performance system”. Four screens went into the kitchen:

1. **Service-speed monitor**: every bowl had to move from order to table within 3 minutes 30 seconds.
2. **Ingredient-waste rate**: the weight of chilli, spices and noodles had to be exact; a deviation above 2% reduced the bonus.
3. **Gross-margin contribution per customer**: the highest-margin topping was automatically promoted at the till.
4. **Composite Ranking Index (CRI)**: cooks were scored on the first three measures; the top 10% received large bonuses and the bottom 10% entered a performance conversation and could be dismissed.

At first the system looked powerful. Service time fell from five minutes to 3:15, waste fell 18%, gross margin rose four percentage points, and all four screens stayed green. At the monthly meeting the owner announced: “Our objective, quantitative model proves that traditional craft can be standardised completely by an algorithm.”

The change began quietly. Chen, the third-generation oil master, noticed that the slow-frying stage had been shortened by one third because longer cooking increased evaporation loss. He warned that the chilli aroma had not developed and only dry heat remained. The owner pointed to the data: the Color Index was exactly 85 and the weight exactly 15 grams. “The data is objective,” he said. “Do not rely on feelings.”

The junior cooks also learned how to play the algorithm. To stay below 3:30 they lifted noodles before the water had fully boiled. To avoid a weight penalty, they used a standard spoon instead of adjusting for noodle thickness. Because high-margin toppings received the highest recommendation weight, servers pushed expensive pork chops on neighbours who had eaten the signature noodles for decades.

By the third month regulars ate quietly and left. The online five-star reviews remained numerous, but repeat visits slid. One rainy day a cargo-ship captain came in to warm himself before sailing. After one bite he asked: “The heat is strong—stronger than before—so why does the bowl feel colder? Where did the warmth go?”

The owner stared at the all-green KPI screens and the number-one ranking sheet. That evening, for the first time in decades, the shop did not close on time. The master cook lifted a spoon of oil and let it fall slowly. “Your first two spoons measured weight, cost and speed. Old Sichuan Notes has lasted a hundred years because of the **third spoon of chilli oil**.”

“What is the third spoon?”

“It is the extra care when a customer walks in through rain and mud; the intention that the bowl will warm someone in a storm; and knowing when to stop the clock so the flavour can be right. A metric recognises the weight in the spoon, not the temperature in a customer’s eyes. If everything unmeasurable is treated as nonexistent, the result is a green but lifeless dashboard.”

The next day the owner removed the single composite ranking. He kept speed and cost monitoring, but changed them from the only steering wheel into guardrails. He restored the old sign: “Make noodles honestly; welcome customers warmly.” Every Friday he sat with the cooks, tasted a fresh bowl, listened to customers and asked which ideas could improve the heat and timing.

Months later service was twenty seconds slower and waste was slightly higher, but the aroma returned. The captain came back, wiped his mouth and smiled: “The soul of this place is back.”

### The judgement to learn today

Management accounting and strategic control are not about designing an apparently flawless score. Any highly simplified quantitative measure can induce gaming; when a measure becomes the target itself, it stops being a good measure (Goodhart’s Law).

Leadership control combines **Diagnostic Controls** with **Interactive Controls**, and **Boundary Systems** with **Belief Systems**.

## Part 2 | Core case and theory

### 1. Tennessee Controls: The Strategic Ranking Problem (HBS 9-191-083)

Tennessee Controls is a diversified industrial-controls manufacturer. To discipline capital-expenditure approval, it created a highly precise **Strategic Ranking Index (SRI)** for dozens of asset-purchase and investment projects.

* Financial measures (NPV, IRR and Payback), strategic fit, technical risk and market growth received different weights and produced one 0–100 score.
* Capital was allocated by cutting the list at a score threshold.

The system failed in four ways:

1. **False precision**: Is an 82-point project truly better than a 79-point project? The score hides fragile assumptions.
2. **Gaming the model**: Managers reverse-engineer the pass mark, inflate sales growth or cost savings, and lower the discount rate.
3. **Ignored interdependencies**: Low-scoring infrastructure or IT projects are cut even though high-scoring front-office projects cannot operate without them.
4. **Death of dialogue**: The committee debates whether strategic fit is 7 or 8 instead of challenging the business logic.

The management lesson is simple: quantitative ranking is a **starting point for dialogue**, never a substitute for commercial judgement.

### 2. Simons’ four levers of control

Robert Simons’ framework says that strong control is not a single KPI score but two balancing pairs:

```text
                 DRIVE STRATEGY AND CREATIVITY
                         /                 \
              Belief Systems        Interactive Controls
              purpose and values     dialogue on uncertainty
                         \                 /
                 Boundary Systems + Diagnostic Controls
                 limits and ethics    budgets, KPIs, variance
                 PROTECT AGAINST RISK AND GUIDE BEHAVIOUR
```

1. **Belief Systems** define purpose, mission and values: why do we exist, and what deserves commitment and exploration?
2. **Boundary Systems** define ethical and risk limits: no bribery, no unhedged exposure beyond mandate, and no falsified environmental data.
3. **Diagnostic Control Systems** use budgets, KPIs, financial statements and variance analysis to monitor execution against known targets.
4. **Interactive Control Systems** involve senior leaders in continuing two-way discussion around strategic uncertainties. The question is whether the original targets and assumptions still hold.

### 3. Further reading | Innovation and purpose

* **Davila (2005), Management Control Systems for Innovation** challenges the idea that control necessarily kills innovation. Early controls should be information tools that clarify learning and reduce the cost of experiments, not only brakes that punish variance.
* **Quinn & Thakor (2018), Creating a Purpose-Driven Organization (HBR)** argues that excessive reliance on bonuses, scores and punishments can produce cynicism. A credible higher purpose can guide decisions when rules do not specify every situation, while still working with boundaries, data and accountability.

## Part 3 | Connection to the Team 6 BeFrank assignment

For Friday’s **BeFrank & Impact Investing** presentation, Simons’ levers help the team examine whether an employer can turn an employee-centred objective into credible provider dialogue and review:

1. **Boundary System**: define the employer’s non-negotiable requirements for pension adequacy, risk, transparency, cost, employee communication and evidence. Do not turn an unverified provider claim into a control boundary.
2. **Diagnostic System**: use a small set of agreed indicators to monitor whether the proposed arrangement delivers the intended employee outcome, remains understandable and stays within the employer’s cost and risk limits.
3. **Interactive System**: do not copy Tennessee Controls’ mechanical scoring trap. The employer should use continuing dialogue with BeFrank to challenge assumptions about employee relevance, investment options, impact evidence and implementation feasibility.
4. **Belief System**: state why the company is considering impact investing for its employees, while keeping the purpose connected to pension adequacy, responsible stewardship and accountability rather than a slogan.
